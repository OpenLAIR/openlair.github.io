Fact-checking peer reviews

PremiseCheck: Efficient Fact-Checking of Human and AI Peer Reviews

An LLM rewrites each review comment as explicit premises without reading the paper, and a cheap decision model checks each premise against the paper.

TL;DR

To check whether a review comment misstates the paper, PremiseCheck lets an LLM rewrite the comment as explicit premises without reading the paper and lets a cheap decision model verify each premise against the paper. When each comment is checked on its own, it reaches accuracy comparable to holistic GPT-5.5 on all three fields of our ReviewFact benchmark at about half its cost, and at a quarter of its cost on NLP reviews.

01Abstract

Can review comments be fact-checked without an LLM reading the paper each time?

Peer review is under strain from the growth of submissions, and review comments written by humans or AI can misstate what a paper says. We introduce ReviewFact, a benchmark that labels such factual errors in review comments under one definition across NLP, machine learning, and the natural sciences, and that measures cost and latency alongside accuracy. A frontier LLM checks comments well but must read the whole paper for each check, while cheap decision models, which score whether a statement holds in a text, fall far short on whole comments that mix several implicit assertions. PremiseCheck is a framework that combines the two. An LLM rewrites each comment as explicit premises without reading the paper, and a decision model checks each premise against the paper. When each comment is checked on its own, PremiseCheck reaches accuracy comparable to GPT-5.5 across all three fields at about half its cost, and on NLP reviews at a quarter of its cost and less than half its latency.

The LLM never reads the paper

It only rewrites the comment, which averages 24 tokens on the NLP reviews, as one to six self-contained premises, explicit and implicit.

A decision model reads it instead

It returns the probability that a statement holds and generates no text. All its calls together cost $0.0015 per comment on the NLP reviews.

Each flag names a premise

A comment gets the score of its weakest premise, so a flagged comment comes with the premise that the decision model finds false.

02The idea

Three ways to check a review comment

Decision model only

A decision model is cheap and fast, but on whole comments it is near chance on the NLP reviews (AUROC 49.9). A comment mixes several assertions, many of them implicit, while a decision model is reliable only on explicit, self-contained claims.

LLM only

A frontier LLM judging each comment holistically is accurate (AUROC 63.2) but must reread the full paper for every comment, a cost that multiplies across tens of thousands of reviews.

PremiseCheck

The LLM turns the comment into explicit premises from the comment alone, and the decision model checks each one against the paper. In this example the premise that the paper tests reproducibility only on the Gopher model gets 0.03, and the comment is caught. On the NLP reviews this setup reaches AUROC 63.1 at 25% of the cost and 2.8 s per comment.

03Method

How PremiseCheck works

PremiseCheck scores a comment on a paper in four steps. Only the first step uses an LLM, and it never sees the paper. Any LLM and any decision model can fill the two roles. Figure 1 shows the four steps on a real comment.

  1. 1LLM

    Decompose

    The LLM reconstructs the comment as an argument with one to six premises. Each is one self-contained declarative sentence that names the method, table or section it refers to, including what a question presupposes about the paper.

  2. 2Decision model

    Route

    From the premise alone, the decision model estimates whether it can be decided from the paper (“the paper does not report results on dataset X”) or relies on general knowledge (“method Y is a standard baseline”).

  3. 3Decision model

    Verify

    The decision model reads the paper with the premise and answers the question that fits its route: “true according to the paper” for paper premises, “true” for knowledge premises.

  4. 4min

    Aggregate

    A comment that rests on a false premise contains a factual error, so the comment score is the lowest premise score. A low score flags the comment for a person to check.

Why it is cheap. The LLM call is short because it excludes the paper. A decision model generates no text, so its cost grows only with the length of its input, and asking several questions about the same input adds almost no cost. In holistic judgment, by contrast, the LLM reads the paper and reasons about it for every comment, so its cost grows with the length of the paper at LLM prices.

04Benchmark

ReviewFact: factual errors in human and AI review comments

ReviewFact asks whether a single review comment says something the paper does not support. It labels such factual errors in human-written and AI-written comments under one definition across three fields, and it measures cost and latency alongside AUROC.

aThe task

A system receives a paper and one comment from a review of it and returns a score between 0 and 1, where a low score means that the comment likely contains a factual error. A factual error says something about the paper that the paper does not support. Following ReviewCritique, the comment either

  • Misunderstandingmisreads the paper,
  • Neglectclaims that the paper lacks something it contains,
  • Unstated statementasserts something with no basis in the paper, or
  • Inaccurate summarymisstates its content.

Errors of judgment, such as whether a concern is important, are outside the task.

bLabels from expert annotations

ReviewFact adds no new comments or papers. It maps the expert judgments of ReviewCritique (NLP), ReviewScore (machine learning) and PeerReview Bench (natural sciences) to one label, and it leaves out comments outside the task.

Human verification. Many PeerReview Bench comments that the experts judged incorrect are errors of judgment, so GPT-5.5 decides from the expert's rationale whether an error is factual. Two authors independently repeat this decision on the 178 comments that have a rationale. They agree with each other at Cohen's κ = 0.70 and with GPT-5.5 at 0.76 and 0.77. With their labels instead, the difference between PremiseCheck and holistic GPT-5.5 ranges from −3.7 to +2.7 and is never significant.

06Findings

What makes PremiseCheck work, and how to use it

Decomposition moves the work that needs reasoning from the LLM to the decision model. The LLM can then run without reasoning, and decision models from other providers, including an open-weight one, can check the premises.

1Finding 1

Decomposition makes decision models useful

  • Every decision model gains from decomposition. Jev, Kev-27B, Microsoft Decision-1 and Perplexity Decider score 50 to 57 as holistic judges but come within one point of holistic GPT-5.5 as verifiers, although the intervals of Kev-27B and Perplexity Decider fall just short of non-inferiority.
  • A cheap verifier is enough. On the same premises, GPT-5.5 as the verifier is no more accurate than Jev but makes the pipeline cost 3.1 times as much as holistic judgment.
  • Much cheaper LLMs can decompose. With Kev-27B as the verifier, GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-5.6 Luna and Nemotron Super 3 are non-inferior to holistic GPT-5.5 at 6% to 17% of the cost of their own holistic judgment, while weaker decomposers lose 2 to 6 points.

2Finding 2

An open-weight decision model works as well

  • Kev-27B instead of Jev. With the open-weight Kev-27B as router and verifier, PremiseCheck reaches the same AUROC as with Jev. With the no-reasoning decomposer it is non-inferior at an API cost of 17.8% of the reference, not counting the GPU time of Kev-27B; with the medium-reasoning decomposer its interval falls just short of non-inferiority.
  • Not a proxy for comment length. Longer comments are more often factual errors, but within groups of similar length and after combining each score with length, PremiseCheck stays within 3 points of holistic GPT-5.5 without a significant difference, though none of these comparisons reaches non-inferiority. Adding PremiseCheck to length raises AUROC significantly, by 3.3.

3Finding 3

Batching helps both approaches, and fusing them gives the best result

When a review is finished, as for an area chair or an author, all its comments can be checked in one call. We batch both approaches per review and fuse them by averaging the percentile ranks of their scores.

Batched holistic GPT-5.5 without reasoning and batched PremiseCheck do not differ significantly, so holistic GPT-5.5 is the cheaper choice in this setting, while PremiseCheck also returns the premises. The two scores have a rank correlation of only 0.56, and their fusion is significantly above batched holistic GPT-5.5.

4Finding 4

Flagging comments for a person to check

  • Flags hit errors two to three times as often as random. This holds for PremiseCheck and batched PremiseCheck among the 5% most suspicious comments, whereas holistic GPT-5.5, per comment or batched, does little better than random despite a similar AUROC.
  • An area chair sees more errors. Reading the three most suspicious comments of each paper finds an error for 40% of the papers that contain one with batched PremiseCheck, compared with 29% with batched holistic GPT-5.5.
  • A flag points to the premise that the paper refutes, as in Figure 9.

Design choices

What each part of the pipeline contributes

  • Routing is needed, but the exact route is not. Kev-27B agrees with Jev on only 65% of the routes yet gives the same AUROC, whereas skipping routing lowers AUROC from 61.8 to 58.8 on a common set of machine learning premises, because knowledge premises then receive low probabilities.
  • The weakest premise decides. Taking the mean over premises instead of the minimum lowers AUROC to 59.4.
  • Contradiction questions add accuracy at no cost. Asking 16 phrasings of the verification question in one decision-model call, half of them whether the paper contradicts the premise, raises AUROC on the NLP reviews from 63.1 to 65.7, a significant gain over the original question.
  • The decomposer needs no reasoning. Without reasoning, per-comment holistic judgment falls to chance level on the NLP reviews, while PremiseCheck keeps its accuracy because the LLM only rewrites the comment.

07Limitations

What PremiseCheck does not solve

  • The task is hard. Each error is something an expert reviewer overlooked or misread, and the evidence against it may lie anywhere in a long paper. On the NLP reviews, per-comment AUROC is 63.2 for holistic GPT-5.5, 63.0 to 64.1 for PremiseCheck in Table 1, and 65.7 with 16 verification phrasings.
  • The machine learning and natural-science parts are small. They contain 46 and 65 factual errors, and with the medium-reasoning decomposer the 95% intervals for the difference to holistic GPT-5.5 are [−9.4, +11.7] and [−8.9, +7.6]. On these parts the difference is not significant; non-inferiority is shown on the NLP reviews only.
  • Facts, not judgment. PremiseCheck treats a comment as wrong when one premise is false, which does not hold for errors of judgment, such as whether a concern matters or how novel the work is. On labels that also count such errors, it falls behind holistic GPT-5.5. Detecting errors of judgment is left for future work.
  • Where it fails. In a sample of failures, false alarms on the NLP reviews come mostly from decomposition (58%), where a premise adds to, redirects or blurs what the reviewer said, and on the natural-science reviews mostly from verification (45%).
  • Model availability. The main decision model, Jev, is served through an API. With the open-weight Kev-27B, PremiseCheck reaches the same AUROC on the NLP reviews; further open decision models are untested.
  • Intended use. A verifier that wrongly flags a correct comment could unfairly discredit a reviewer, and one that misses an error could lend false authority to a wrong comment. PremiseCheck is meant to direct human attention to comments that may misstate the paper, not to replace the judgment of reviewers, area chairs or authors.

Release. The NLP and natural-science parts of ReviewFact are released under the licenses of ReviewCritique and PeerReview Bench, which allow evaluation but not training. ReviewScore states no license, so for the machine learning part we release a script that rebuilds it from the original data. The code is released under the MIT license.
